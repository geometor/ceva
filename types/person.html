Types / person

Content of type 'person'

Apollonius of Perga

Author of the Conics, celebrated in Ceva’s Prooemium and applied across Liber Secundus and the Appendix Geometrica.

Archimedes of Syracuse

Author of On the Equilibrium of Planes (Equiponderants I.6–7), Quadrature of the Parabola, and On the Sphere and Cylinder; foundational authority for Ceva’s Statica Constructio.

August Ferdinand Möbius

Author of Der barycentrische Calcul (1827), formalizing Ceva’s positive and negative static weights (F - A) into homogeneous barycentric coordinates.

Bonaventura Cavalieri, O.S.H.

Author of Geometria indivisibilibus continuorum (1635); credited in Ceva’s Prooemium with curing his youthful circle-quadrature obstinacy, and critically bounded on curved surfaces at the close of the Appendix Geometrica.

Christian Heinrich von Nagel

Investigator of the excircle-contact point $X(8)$, isotomic conjugate of $X(7)$ via Ceva’s Liber Secundus, Prop. I Corollarium.

Christoph Clavius, S.J.

Jesuit mathematician of the Collegio Romano, cited by Ceva in Liber Primus on compound-ratio manipulation (Euclidis Elementorum Libri XV).

Clark Kimberling

Creator of the Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers (ETC, geometor/etc), indexing tens of thousands of barycentric and isotomic concurrencies governed by Ceva’s Theorem.

Claudius Ptolemy

Author of the Almagest (Book I.13), transmitting the planar and spherical Menelaus sector configuration in compound-ratio form.

Donato Rossetti

Canon of Livorno and Professor at the University of Pisa, gratefully honored in Ceva’s 1678 Prooemium as his beloved teacher.

Euclid of Alexandria

Author of the Elements (Stoicheia), Books I, V, VI, XI, and XII, supplying the proportional and area-ratio foundations of Caravaggio Jr.’s Prop. VI and Ceva’s Appendix Geometrica.

Evangelista Torricelli

Author of Opera geometrica (1644), cited in Ceva’s Appendix Geometrica on the $2 : 1$ paraboloid centroid and the acute infinite hyperbolic solid.

Federico Commandino

Translator of Euclid, Archimedes, Apollonius, Serenus (1566), and Pappus (1588), and author of Liber de centro gravitatis solidorum (1565); cited by Ceva on compound ratios and solid centroids.

Ferdinando Carlo Gonzaga-Nevers

Tenth Duke of Mantua, Montferrat, and Guastalla, patron and dedicatee of Ceva’s 1678 treatise.

Giovanni Alfonso Borelli

Pisan mathematician and editor of Apollonius Books V–VII (1661), mentor of Donato Rossetti, and cited by Ceva in Liber Secundus, Lemma I.

Giovanni Ceva

Milanese geometer, statician, and court mathematician to Ferdinando Carlo Gonzaga at Mantua; author of De lineis rectis se invicem secantibus statica constructio (Milan, 1678), Opuscula mathematica (1682), Geometria motus (1692), and De re numaria (1711). Transformed Archimedes’ Law of the Lever into a general projective method of geometric incidence and ratio analysis.

Hippocrates of Chios

Pioneer of curvilinear lune quadrature, re-proved synthetically by Ceva in Appendix Geometrica, Proposition II.

Joseph Diez Gergonne

Projective geometer after whom the incircle-contact concurrency point $X(7)$ (proved in Ceva’s 1678 Liber Secundus, Prop. I) is named.

Lazare Nicolas Marguerite Carnot

Author of Essai sur la théorie des transversales (1806), reviving and extending Ceva’s and Menelaus’s theorems across polygons and algebraic curves.

Luca Valerio

Author of De centro gravitatis solidorum libri tres (1604), hailed by Ceva in Appendix Geometrica as “subtilissimus nostri æui Archimedes” for the $5 : 3$ hemisphere centroid.

Ludovico Monza

Master Milanese printer (Ex Typographia Ludovici Montiae) of the 1678 editio princeps, noted for setting algebraic addition with the obelus/dagger † (A † F).

Menelaus of Alexandria

Author of the Sphaerica (Book III, Lemma 1), whose collinear transversal theorem Ceva re-proves by two-lever statics in Liber Primus, Elementum I and Proposition VIII.

Pappus of Alexandria

Author of the Synagoge (Books VII & VIII), preserving ancient projective harmonic invariance and mechanics.

Pietro Paolo Caravaggio Jr.

Son of Ceva’s Milanese mentor Pietro Paolo Caravaggio Sr., credited in Liber Primus, Proposition VI (tab. 1. fig. 10) with the pure Euclidean parallel-line proof of cevian concurrency.

Serenus of Antinoöpolis

Author of On the Section of a Cylinder, cited by Ceva in Liber Secundus, Lemma I to project tangent ratios from circles onto ellipses.

Tommaso Ceva, S.J.

Younger brother of Giovanni Ceva, celebrated Jesuit mathematician, humanist, and poet; long-serving professor of mathematics at the Jesuit Brera College in Milan, inventor of the mathematical trisection instrument (pantograph-like gravity compass), and author of Opuscula mathematica (1699).

Yusuf al-Mu’taman ibn Hud

Hudid King of Zaragoza whose Arabic Kitāb al-Istikmāl contained the earliest surviving synthetic proof of the cevian concurrency theorem (unknown in Latin Europe and rediscovered by J. P. Hogendijk in 1985).
