Lemma III & Proposition VI: Six Cases of Element I (Caravaggio Proof)¶

Lemma III.

(Tab. 1, Fig. 9) Let \(B\) be the center of gravity of weights \(A, C\). I say that weight \(B\)—namely the aggregate of the heavy bodies \(A, C\) weighing at \(B\)—is to weight \(C\) as \(AC\) to \(BA\). Since \(B\) is the center of gravity of weights \(A, C\), \(A\) will be to \(C\) as \(CB\) to \(BA\); therefore by compounding, weight \(B\) will be to \(C\) as \(CA\) to \(BA\), which &c.

Proposition VI · Problem VI

Having set forth the figure A E C F of the First Element, let weights A, E, C be understood as disposed at A, E, C in that manner in which the weights \(G, I, H\) were set forth there, so that D is the center of gravity of weights A, E, B of weights A, C, and F of all; therefore there will be four ratios, \(AB\) to \(BC\), \(AD\) to \(DE\), \(EF\) to \(FB\), and \(CF\) to \(FD\), of which if any two be given in numbers, we shall investigate the remaining two.

Of this problem there are six cases; for (as from the combinatorial art) multiplying four (that is, how many ratios are given) by three, namely the number deficient by unity, there arises \(12\), half of which, \(6\), furnishes us the number of pairs into which the aforesaid number \(4\) can be distributed.

(Tab. 1, Fig. 4) Let there be given first two ratios \(EF\) to \(FB\) and \(CF\) to \(FD\), let the former be as \(2\) to \(1\), and the latter as \(3\) to \(2\); we must now make manifest the remaining two ratios \(CB\) to \(BA\) and \(ED\) to \(DA\).

Since weight B (namely the aggregate of A, C) is to C (by the premised Third Lemma) as straight line \(AC\) to \(AB\), while the ratio of weight B to C is compounded of the ratios of weights B to F and F to C, and as B to F, so \(EF\) to \(EB\), and as F to C, so \(DC\) to \(DF\), the ratio \(AC\) to \(AB\) will be compounded of the ratios \(EF\) to \(EB\) and \(DC\) to \(DF\); and since \(EF\) to \(FB\) is as \(2\) to \(1\), by compounding, then by conversion of ratio, and by converting, \(EF\) to \(EB\) is as \(2\) to \(3\); and \(CF\) to \(FD\) was also as \(3\) to \(2\), and by compounding \(CD\) to \(FD\) as \(5\) to \(2\); therefore straight line \(AC\) to \(AB\) will be compounded of the ratios \(2\) to \(3\) and \(5\) to \(2\), or of these, \(2\) to \(3\) and \(3\) to \(1\tfrac{1}{5}\); that is, the same \(AC\) to \(AB\) as \(2\) to \(1\tfrac{1}{5}\), or as \(10\) to \(6\); wherefore by dividing, \(CB\) to \(BA\) will be as \(4\) to \(6\), or as \(2\) to \(3\). By the same reason, since weight D to E is compounded of the ratios of weights D to F and F to E, namely of straight lines \(CF\) to \(CD\) and \(EB\) to \(BF\), the same weight D to E, that is, \(AE\) to \(DA\), will be compounded of the ratios \(3\) to \(5\) and \(3\) to \(1\): and of these ratios is compounded that \(3\) to \(1\tfrac{2}{3}\), that is, \(9\) to \(5\); therefore \(AE\) to \(DA\) is as \(9\) to \(5\), but by dividing, \(ED\) to \(DA\) will be as \(4\) to \(5\); therefore \(CB\) to \(BA\) is as \(2\) to \(3\), and \(ED\) to \(DA\) as \(4\) to \(5\), which was to be done.

II. Let two ratios be given, \(EF\) to \(FB\) as \(4\) to \(5\), and \(ED\) to \(DA\) as \(7\) to \(9\); we must trace out the remaining ratios \(CF\) to \(FD\) and \(CB\) to \(BA\).

The ratio of straight line \(AC\) to \(CB\)—namely of weight B to A—is compounded of the ratios of weights B to E and E to A, namely of straight lines \(EF\) to \(FB\) and \(AD\) to \(DE\), that is, of the ratios \(4\) to \(5\) and \(9\) to \(7\); wherefore \(AC\) to \(CB\) is compounded of the ratios \(4\) to \(5\) and \(9\) to \(7\): now this compounded ratio is that which \(4\) has to \(3\tfrac{8}{9}\), or \(36\) to \(35\); therefore \(AC\) to \(CB\) is as \(36\) to \(35\), and by dividing, \(AB\) to \(BC\) as \(1\) to \(35\).

Similarly, since \(CD\) to \(CF\)—that is, weight F to D—is compounded of the ratios of weights F to E and E to D, namely of straight lines \(EB\) to \(BF\) and \(DA\) to \(AE\), that is, of the ratios \(9\) to \(5\) and \(9\) to \(16\); now this ratio is the same as that which \(9\) has to \(8\tfrac{8}{9}\), or as \(81\) to \(80\); by dividing, \(DF\) to \(FC\) will be as \(1\) to \(80\).

  1. But if two ratios be given, \(CF\) to \(FD\) as \(4\) to \(5\), and \(CB\) to \(BA\) as \(7\) to \(9\), we shall likewise give, in the same way as above, \(AD\)

to \(DE\) as \(1\) to \(35\); and \(BF\) to \(FE\) as \(1\) to \(80\).

IV. Let the ratio \(ED\) to \(DA\) be as \(20\) to \(21\), and \(CB\) to \(BA\) as \(99\) to \(25\), and let it be required to make known the two ratios \(EF\) to \(FB\) and \(CF\) to \(FD\). The ratio of straight line \(CF\) to \(FD\)—namely of weight D to C—is compounded of the ratios of weights D to A and A to C, that is, of straight lines \(EA\) to \(ED\) and \(CB\) to \(BA\), namely of the ratios \(41\) to \(20\) and \(99\) to \(25\): but \(41\) to \(5\tfrac{5}{99}\) is compounded of the same ratios; therefore \(CF\) to \(DF\) is as \(41\) to \(5\tfrac{5}{99}\), that is, as \(4059\) to \(500\).

By the same reason, since \(EF\) to \(FB\)—that is, weight B to E—is compounded of the ratios of weights B to A and A to E, namely of the lines \(CA\) to \(CB\) and \(ED\) to \(DA\), that is, of the ratios \(124\) to \(99\) and \(20\) to \(21\); and since of the same ratios is compounded the ratio which \(124\) has to \(103\tfrac{19}{20}\), that is, as \(2480\) to \(2079\), \(EF\) to \(FB\) will be in the same ratio.

V. Let two proportions be given, \(ED\) to \(DA\) as \(2\) to \(1\), and \(CF\) to \(FD\) of equality; we must investigate the remaining two, namely \(CB\) to \(BA\) and \(EF\) to \(FB\). Compounded is \(CB\) to \(BA\)—doubtless weight A to C—of the ratios of weights A to D and D to C, namely of straight lines \(ED\) to \(EA\) and \(CF\) to \(FD\); that is, of the ratios \(2\) to \(3\) and \(3\) to \(3\); therefore \(CB\) to \(BA\) is as \(2\) to \(3\).

Similarly \(EB\) to \(FB\)—that is, the ratio of weight F to E—is compounded of the ratios of weights F to D, D to E, namely of straight lines \(CD\) to \(CF\) and \(AE\) to \(AD\), that is, of the ratios \(2\) to \(1\) and \(3\) to \(1\); but of the same ratios is compounded \(2\) to \(\tfrac{1}{3}\), or \(6\) to \(1\); therefore \(EB\) to \(BF\) is as \(6\) to \(1\), and by dividing, \(EF\) to \(FB\) as \(5\) to \(1\).

VI. And lastly. If the given proportions be \(CB\) to \(BA\) as \(2\) to \(1\), and \(EF\) to \(FB\) of equality, we shall show (as was done above) \(ED\) to \(DA\) as \(2\) to \(3\), and \(CF\) to \(FD\) as \(5\) to \(1\).

Scholium.

As often as a problem is impossible, it will be discerned from the operation itself; for if the ratio \(DF\) to \(FC\) be given as a ratio of equality, and likewise \(BF\) to \(FE\) of equality, I say that the question is impossible. For since weight C to B, that is, straight line \(AB\) to \(AC\), is compounded of the ratios of weights C to F and F to B, namely of straight lines \(DF\) to \(DC\) and \(EB\) to \(EF\), that is, of the ratios \(1\) to \(2\) and \(2\) to \(1\), the part \(AB\) to the whole \(AC\) will be as \(1\) to \(1\), the part equal to the whole, which is absurd. This, or a not dissimilar absurdity, always

f. 0027 · p. 10

follows whenever the proposed question is impossible.

The first part of this proposition, or the first case, whose title I had transmitted to my friend without adding or indicating any solution, was demonstrated geometrically by a noble youth, conspicuous for his multitude of languages, arts, and sciences, Pietro Paolo Caravaggio, son of my teacher Pietro Paolo. I set down his demonstration, which will place this static method in a geometric light.

Let \(EF\) to \(FB\) be as \(2\) to \(1\), and \(CF\) to \(FD\) as \(3\) to \(2\); I say that \(ED\) to \(DA\) is as \(4\) to \(5\), and \(CB\) to \(BA\) as \(2\) to \(3\).*

(Tab. 1, Fig. 9, emend. Fig. 10) Let GH be drawn parallel to EC. Since EC to \(GF\) is as \(CD\) to \(DF\), namely as \(5\) to \(2\), and EC to \(FH\) as \(15\) to \(5\), EC to GH will be as \(15\) to \(11\); and equally AE to \(AG\) and AC to \(AH\) as \(15\) to \(11\); therefore of those parts of which AE is \(15\), \(GE\) will be \(4\), and likewise of those parts of which AC is \(15\), \(HC\) will be \(4\); since therefore \(GE\) to \(ED\) is as \(CF\) to \(CD\), namely as \(3\) to \(5\), if as \(3\) to \(5\), so \(4\) be made to another number, the number \(\tfrac{20}{3}\) will come forth for \(ED\), whose remainder from \(EA\) (\(15\)) will be \(\tfrac{25}{3}\); therefore \(ED\) to \(DA\) will be as \(4\) to \(5\).

Similarly since, of those parts of which AC is \(15\), \(CH\) is \(4\), and \(CH\) to \(CB\) is as \(2\) to \(3\), \(CB\) will be \(6\), and \(BA\) \(9\): wherefore \(CB\) to \(BA\) will be as \(2\) to \(3\).
