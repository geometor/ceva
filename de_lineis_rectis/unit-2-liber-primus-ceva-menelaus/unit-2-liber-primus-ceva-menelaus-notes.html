Unit II · Historical & Mathematical Commentary¶

1. Why Ceva Credited Pietro Paolo Caravaggio Jr. in Proposition VI¶

In seventeenth-century Milan, mathematics flourished around the Palatine Schools (Scuole Palatine) and the Jesuit Brera College (where Giovanni’s brother Tommaso Ceva taught). Pietro Paolo Caravaggio Sr. (1617–1688) held the chair of mathematics at the Palatine Schools, and his teenage prodigy son Pietro Paolo Caravaggio Jr. (1658–1723) later succeeded him. By explicitly naming Caravaggio Jr. for the Euclidean parallel-line proof of Proposition VI (Tab. 1, Fig. 10), Ceva accomplished a double rhetorical aim: honoring his Milanese teacher’s family while proving beyond cavil that every theorem discovered via Statica Constructio possesses a rigorous, weight-free Euclidean counterpart.

2. Collinearity of $X(1), X(2), X(8)$ on the Nagel Line ($y = 4$)¶

Notice a delightful arithmetic gem revealed by our exact Heronian $\triangle(13, 14, 15)$ table in geometor.ceva:

  • $X(1) = (6, 4)$,

  • $X(2) = \left(\frac{19}{3}, 4\right)$,

  • $X(8) = (7, 4)$.

All three points share the exact integer ordinate $y = 4$ (equal to the inradius $r = \frac{\Delta}{s} = \frac{84}{21} = 4$, since base $BC$ lies along $y = 0$ and $\frac{a \cdot 12}{a + b + c} = \frac{14 \cdot 12}{42} = 4$), and satisfy the universal Nagel-line proportion:

$$X(8) - X(2) = \left(\frac{2}{3}, 0\right) = 2\left(\frac{1}{3}, 0\right) = 2\bigl(X(2) - X(1)\bigr).$$

3. Complete Quadrangle & Projective Invariance of Ceva–Menelaus¶

Viewed in $\mathbb{P}^2$, the four points $(A, B, C, P)$ form a complete quadrangle whose three diagonal points are $(D, E, F)$ (with $D = AP \cap BC$, $E = BP \cap CA$, $F = CP \cap AB$). By Pappus Book VII (1588), the diagonal line $EF$ cuts $BC$ at the fourth harmonic point $D'$ of $(B, C; D)$. Thus Ceva’s Proposition VII and Proposition VIII already encapsulate the fundamental harmonic theorem of synthetic projective geometry later systematized by Lazare Carnot (1806) and Joseph Diez Gergonne.
