Closing Scholium on Indivisibles, Errata & Printer’s Note on ✠¶

SCHOLIUM — A Epistemological Caution on Cavalieri’s Indivisibles (pp. 81–83, Tab. 10, Fig. 84)

Praising Luca Valerio as “the subtlest Archimedes of our age” (subtilissimus nostri æui Archimedes) and acknowledging Cavalieri's method of indivisibles as a marvelous shortcut for volumes of irregular solids, Ceva issues a celebrated methodological warning (indivisibilia cautio):

> “Yet one must proceed with caution, for it happens not rarely that Cavalieri’s reasoning altogether fails—especially when treating the **curved surfaces* of round solids: behold an example!”*

Ceva’s Sphere-Surface Paradox (Tab. 10, Fig. 84): Cut a sphere ACBD (axis AB) by parallel planes perpendicular to AB giving circles of diameters CDEFGH. Since circumferences stand directly as diameters, naive summation of all circumferences would falsely claim that the spherical cap surface EBF stands to the entire sphere surface in the exact same ratio as the circular segment EBF stands to the entire disk ACBD! Yet by Archimedes (On the Sphere and Cylinder), the true surface ratio is $\overline{AB}^2 : \overline{BE}^2$ (equal to $\triangle ABE : \triangle EIB$), which Ceva proves is strictly smaller than the ratio of the circle to its segment EBF! Paradoxically, the exact same naive reasoning does yield a true result when applied to a cone (where the slant angle is constant). Ceva concludes on p. 83:

> “Hence you see, in measuring the surfaces of round solids, how uncertain is the otherwise most ingenious method of indivisibles.” — F I N I S.

Errata / Correctiones & Ludovico Monza’s Printer’s Note on ``✠`` (p. 83, folio 0100): Directly below F I N I S., the 1678 edition prints a 20-line Errata / Correctiones table (including critical marginal figure fixes on pp. 10, 12, and 51), followed by the famous typographic note explaining Ceva’s plus symbol (obelus additionis):

> “To express **plus* in the algebraic manner, for want of the appropriate sign +, we have used this other sign, although not customary, ✠, which we also not rarely employ to supplement the letters.”*
