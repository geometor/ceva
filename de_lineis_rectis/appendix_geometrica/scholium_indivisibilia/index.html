Closing Scholium on Indivisibles, Errata & Printer’s Note on ✠¶

SCHOLIUM.

These theorems, several of which I had deduced from geometric principles, I have demonstrated more expeditiously by Cavalieri’s method, although Luca Valerio, the subtlest Archimedes of our age, long ago explored geometrically the centers of gravity of a hemisphere and a parabolic conoid. I confess that the method of indivisibles is a great shortcut in geometry, especially in measuring solids, however irregular—a work full of windings if we wish to use geometric reasonings. Yet one must proceed cautiously, for it happens not rarely that Cavalieri’s reasoning fails altogether, especially regarding surfaces of round solids: behold an example.

Let there be a sphere whose axis is AB, cut by any planes perpendicular to the axis CD, EF, GH, etc. (margin: Tab. 10, Fig. 84); I shall say that the whole surface of the sphere has to its portion EBF the same ratio which the circle has to its segment EBF. For it is evident that all sections CD, EF, GH, etc. are circles about diameters CD, EF, GH, etc. parallel to one another; wherefore the circumferences of the same circles will also be equidistant from one another, and likewise the diameters will be parallel to one another. And because the circumference about diameter CD is to that

f. 0099 · p. 82

about diameter EF as diameter CD is to EF, and likewise the circumference about diameter EF is to the circumference about diameter GH as diameter EF is to diameter GH, jointly all circumferences about CD, EF, HG will be to all circumferences about EF, GH, etc. as all diameters CD, FE, HG, etc. are to all diameters EF, GH, etc.: that is, the whole surface of the sphere AEBF will have to its part EBF the same ratio which the circle has to its segment EBF, which however is false; for circle ACBD has to its segment EBF, less than a semicircle, a greater ratio than square AB has to the square of straight line BE, that is, than the surface of the sphere has to its portion EBF; for the circle whose radius is AB is equal to the whole surface of the sphere, whereas the circle whose radius is BE is equal to surface EBF of the same sphere; wherefore the surface of the sphere is to its portion EBF as the square on AB is to the square on BE, or as triangle ABE is to triangle EIB: now circle AB is posited to be in the same ratio to its segment EBF, or semicircle AEB to trilineal figure EBI; therefore the remaining spaces will also be in the same ratio: that is, segments AEC, EBG together, to segment EBG, will be as triangle ABE to triangle EIB, or again as square AB to square BE, indeed as the aggregate of squares AE, EB to EB itself; but the segment of a circle whose base is AE, similar to EBG, falls inside segment ACE; therefore segment ACE will have to segment EGB a greater ratio than square AE to square EB, and jointly the two segments AEC, EBG will also have to segment EBG a greater proportion than the two squares AE, EB together, that is, AB itself to square EB, or than triangle AEB to EBI itself; wherefore the two antecedents together to the two consequents together, that is, the semicircle to trilineal figure AGBI, or double to double, namely the circle to its segment EBF, will have a greater ratio than triangle AEB to EBI itself, or greater than square AB to BE, which was to be shown.

On the contrary, if you substitute a cone for the sphere in the aforesaid reasoning, you will deduce the truth: so that the whole conical surface is to its part cut off between the vertex of the cone and a plane parallel to its base as the triangle through the axis is to that triangle intercepted between the said vertex and the aforesaid plane, which

f. 0100 · p. 83

is a part of the same triangle through the axis.

Hence you see, in measuring the surfaces of round solids, how uncertain is the otherwise most ingenious method of indivisibles.

F I N I S.
