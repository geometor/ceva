Unit IV · Historical & Mathematical Commentary
==============================================

1. Ceva's Cylindrical *Ungula* (1678) and Archimedes' Lost *Method* (1906)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

In *Appendix Geometrica, Proposition VII*, :person:`Giovanni Ceva <ceva>` proves that a cylindrical hoof (:term:`ungula cylindrica <ungula-cylindrica>`) cut by a plane through a diameter of the base has volume $\frac{2}{3} R^2 H$—remarkably free of $\pi$ and equal to $\frac{2}{3}$ of its circumscribed triangular prism. Neither Ceva nor his contemporaries knew that :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` had proved this very theorem by mechanical lever slices and triangular cross-sections in Propositions 12–15 of *The Method of Mechanical Theorems*—a treatise lost until Johan Ludvig Heiberg deciphered the Archimedes Palimpsest in Constantinople in **1906**! That Ceva combined mechanical lever equilibrium (*Libri I–II*) with triangular *ungula* cubature (*Appendix Prop. VII*) in the same 1678 volume stands as one of the uncanny resonances in the history of mathematics.

2. Epistemological Significance of Ceva's Indivisibles Paradox
--------------------------------------------------------------

Why did Ceva's closing critique of :person:`Bonaventura Cavalieri's <cavalieri>` indivisibles resonate so strongly across late-seventeenth-century Italy?

* For **volumes** ($\int A(z)\,dz$) and **planar areas** ($\int \ell(y)\,dy$), slicing parallel to a fixed direction preserves Lebesgue/Riemann measure up to a constant shear factor (Cavalieri's Principle).
* For **arc lengths** and **curved surface areas**, however, Hausdorff measure depends on the **metric tangent slope** ($ds = \sqrt{1 + r'(z)^2}\,dz$). Approximating a smooth curve by a staircase of horizontal and vertical steps—or approximating a sphere by stacked cylindrical ribbons of height $dz$ instead of inscribed conical frusta of slant height $ds$—fails to converge in arc/area norm (the classic staircase / Schwarz lantern phenomenon).

By diagnosing this exact failure in 1678—just as Leibniz and Newton were forging the differential $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$—Ceva honored both Cavalieri's heuristic power and the unassailable rigor of :person:`Archimedes <archimedes>` and :person:`Luca Valerio <valerio>`.
