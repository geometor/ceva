**SCHOLIUM — A Epistemological Caution on Cavalieri's Indivisibles** (pp. 81–83, `Tab. 10, Fig. 84`)

Praising :person:`Luca Valerio <valerio>` as *"the subtlest Archimedes of our age"* (*subtilissimus nostri æui Archimedes*) and acknowledging :person:`Cavalieri's <cavalieri>` method of indivisibles as a marvelous shortcut for volumes of irregular solids, Ceva issues a celebrated methodological warning (:term:`indivisibilia cautio <indivisibilia-cautio>`):

> *"Yet one must proceed with caution, for it happens not rarely that Cavalieri's reasoning altogether fails—especially when treating the **curved surfaces** of round solids: behold an example!"*

**Ceva's Sphere-Surface Paradox** (`Tab. 10, Fig. 84`): Cut a sphere :g:`ACBD` (axis :g:`AB`) by parallel planes perpendicular to :g:`AB` giving circles of diameters :g:`CD, EF, GH, ...`. Since circumferences stand directly as diameters, naive summation of all circumferences would falsely claim that the spherical cap surface :g:`EBF` stands to the entire sphere surface in the exact same ratio as the circular segment :g:`EBF` stands to the entire disk :g:`ACBD`! Yet by Archimedes (*On the Sphere and Cylinder*), the true surface ratio is $\overline{AB}^2 : \overline{BE}^2$ (equal to $\triangle ABE : \triangle EIB$), which Ceva proves is strictly *smaller* than the ratio of the circle to its segment :g:`EBF`! Paradoxically, the exact same naive reasoning *does* yield a true result when applied to a **cone** (where the slant angle is constant). Ceva concludes on p. 83:

> *"Hence you see, in measuring the surfaces of round solids, how uncertain is the otherwise most ingenious method of indivisibles."* — **F I N I S.**

**Errata / Correctiones & Ludovico Monza's Printer's Note on ``✠``** (p. 83, folio ``0100``): Directly below **F I N I S.**, the 1678 edition prints a 20-line *Errata / Correctiones* table (including critical marginal figure fixes on pp. 10, 12, and 51), followed by the famous typographic note explaining Ceva's plus symbol (:term:`obelus additionis <obelus-additio>`):

> *"To express **plus** in the algebraic manner, for want of the appropriate sign ``+``, we have used this other sign, although not customary, ``✠``, which we also not rarely employ to supplement the letters."*
